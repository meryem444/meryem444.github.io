Your game plan — the 6-step framework

Memorize this spine. It works for any prompt they throw at you. The mnemonic: F-U-J-I-S-V → "Frame Users' Journey, Ideate, Sketch, Validate."

Golden rule: Never jump to drawing screens. The most common failure is designing a solution before understanding the problem. Spend the first third of your time on Frame + Users + Journey before you sketch anything.
1

Frame the problem · ~5 min · clarify & scope

Restate the prompt in your own words. Ask clarifying questions. Agree on scope, the goal, and one success metric. Write assumptions in a corner of the board.

"Let me make sure I understand — you'd like me to design [restate]. Before I dive in, a few quick questions so I design the right thing… And can I check my assumptions with you as I go?"
2

Users · ~5 min · who & why

List the possible users. Pick one primary persona and say why. Capture their context, goals, and top pain points. In the dental world this is powerful — name a real user like "a general dentist between patients" or "a lab technician."

"There are a few users here — the dentist, the specialist, the lab, the patient. I'll focus on the general dentist because they're the highest-frequency user. Does that fit how you'd scope it?"
3

Journey · ~5–7 min · map the flow

Map the user's journey or the "job to be done" as a horizontal strip of steps. List the key use cases. Prioritize one to design in depth. This is the exact skill the role asks for: mapping complex workflows.

"Here's the current journey, start to finish… I see the biggest friction at this step, so that's where I'll focus my design. Sound right to you all?"
4

Ideate · ~5 min · diverge then converge

Rapidly list 3–5 solution ideas — quantity first, no judgment. Then converge: plot them roughly on impact vs. effort and pick what you'll design. Show you can prioritize, not just generate.

"Let me get a few options on the board first… Now, weighing impact against effort, I'd start with this one because it solves the core pain with the least complexity."
5

Sketch · ~10–12 min · the key screens

Draw the 2–4 key screens or the core flow for your chosen idea. Boxes = screens, arrows = flow. Narrate every box as you draw it. Don't polish — annotate. Invite reactions between screens.

"So the dentist lands here, sees X, taps this, and now they're in Y… I'm keeping this deliberately rough — the thinking matters more than the pixels. What's your reaction so far?"
6

Validate & wrap · ~5 min · edge cases, testing, next steps

Name 1–2 edge cases and how you'd handle them. Say how you'd test/measure success. Summarize your decision and what you'd do next with more time. End with confidence.

"Edge cases I'd handle next: […]. I'd validate this with 5 dentists and watch task-completion time. To recap: I focused on [user], solved [problem] with [solution]. With more time I'd explore […]."

Whiteboard fundamentals — the mechanics

Everything nobody tells you: how to stand, how to divide the board, how to draw, and how to make a room of people feel like collaborators.

Own the board: give it zones

Before you write anything, mentally (or literally) divide the whiteboard into zones. This alone signals "I'm organized" and stops you from making a chaotic mess. Lightly label them as you go.

① Problem & goalRestated prompt + the one success metric. Top-left.
Assumptions / parking lotThings you're assuming or questions to revisit. Top-right corner.
Main design canvasYour journey strip along the top, then your key screen sketches below. This is where you spend most of your time. Keep it generous.
② Primary userWho you're designing for + their top pain.
③ Ideas / priorityYour 3–5 ideas; circle the one you chose.
Why it works: Zones let the interviewers follow your logic like a story. When you say "let me park that assumption up here," you look like someone who runs real design sessions.

How to move & hold the room

  • Stand to the side of what you're drawing so everyone can see. Never block the board with your back for long.
  • Talk to people, draw to the board. Turn to face the room when you're making a point; turn to the board when you're sketching. Don't mumble into the wall.
  • Think out loud, always. Silence is the enemy — they can't score thinking they can't hear. Narrate even your pauses: "Let me think about the edge case here for a second."
  • Use the marker as a pointer. Tap the box you're talking about. It keeps the room's eyes with you.
  • Pace yourself. It's fine to step back, look at the board, and say "okay, where are we." Calm reads as senior.

How to draw (badly, on purpose)

  • A screen is just a rectangle. Add a header bar, a couple of lines for content, a box for the primary action. That's enough.
  • Arrows show flow between screens. Number your screens 1→2→3.
  • Write big and legible. Print, don't cursive. Fewer words, bigger.
  • Label everything. An annotated rough box beats a beautiful unlabeled one.
  • Use a second color (if markers allow) for emphasis, feedback, or edge cases — not decoration.
  • Leave whitespace. Don't cram. You can always erase the parking lot later for room.
Remember: "sloppy but clear" wins. They explicitly are not judging art.

Make it collaborative (this is the whole point here)

The email says "discussion with my colleagues." The people in the room may play stakeholders, or may genuinely co-design. Your job is to make them want to build with you. Concretely:

  • Check in at every phase transition: "Does that assumption hold?" / "Before I sketch — anyone want to steer which user we focus on?"
  • Treat questions as gifts, not attacks. "Great question — let me think about that out loud."
  • Build on their input visibly. If someone suggests something, write it on the board and credit it: "I like that — let me add it here."
  • Ask them as the domain experts. They know dentistry better than you. "You'd know better than me — how often does a dentist actually do this?" This is a strength move, not weakness. It shows humility + collaboration.
  • Disagree gracefully. "That's a fair point. The trade-off I'd weigh is… what would you prioritize?"
The hire signal: By the end, they should be able to picture a Tuesday-morning workshop with you at the board and think "yes, I want that person running our design discussions."

Before you touch the marker — the 60-second ritual

  1. Breathe and restate. Repeat the prompt back. Buys thinking time and confirms you heard it right.
  2. Set the contract. "Here's how I'll approach this — I'll clarify, pick a user, map the flow, then sketch. I'll think out loud and check in with you. Sound good?" — This frames the whole session and calms you.
  3. Write the goal + a metric in the top-left zone. Now you have an anchor you can always return to.

Full worked example — start to finish

A realistic dental-flavored prompt, walked through with the exact words and sketches. Read it once slowly, then try to reproduce the shape of it out loud.

THE PROMPT
"Design a feature that helps a general dentist review a new patient's dental images and quickly decide what needs attention."
Realistic for Medicim/DTX — this is exactly the kind of clinical-workflow problem their software solves.

Step 1  Frame the problem

What you do: restate, ask clarifying questions, lock scope + metric.

"So I'm designing a way for a general dentist to look at a new patient's images and rapidly figure out what needs attention. Before I start, a few questions:
• What images do we have — 2D X-rays, 3D/CBCT scans, intraoral photos, or all of it?
• Is this the dentist's first look at a brand-new patient, or a returning check-up?
• Are we on a desktop chairside, or reviewing beforehand?
• What does 'needs attention' mean to them — clinical findings, or also admin like missing records?"

Then assume & move: "I'll assume desktop, chairside, a new patient with a 2D X-ray set plus intraoral photos, and 'attention' means clinical findings. Success metric: the dentist can form a first assessment in under 60 seconds." (Write goal + metric top-left.)

Step 2  Users

"The users could be the dentist, a hygienist, or an assistant prepping the room. I'll design for the general dentist — they make the call and they're time-pressured between patients. Their goal: build an accurate picture fast, without missing anything. Their pain: images are scattered, and scanning everything manually is slow and error-prone."

Write in the "primary user" zone: 👤 General dentist · goal: fast + accurate first read · pain: scattered data, easy to miss things.

Step 3  Journey — map the flow

Draw a horizontal strip across the top of your canvas:

Open patient → [ See all images in one place ] → Spot problem areas → Confirm/annotate → Decide next step → Note for treatment plan
"The friction is right here — seeing everything in one place and spotting what matters. Today that's manual. That's where I'll focus. Does that match where you see the real pain?"

Step 4  Ideate → prioritize

List 3–5 ideas fast, then pick:

  • A unified "case overview" screen showing all images together.
  • Auto-flagged areas of interest (AI-assisted highlights) the dentist confirms or dismisses.
  • A side-by-side compare view (e.g., against prior visit).
  • A quick checklist the dentist ticks as they review.
"Weighing impact vs. effort, I'll design the unified case-overview screen with confirmable flagged areas — it directly attacks the 'see everything + spot problems fast' pain. The compare-view and checklist I'd add as follow-ups."

Tip: mentioning AI-assisted flagging is a smart nod — DEXIS/DTX genuinely invests in AI findings. But keep the human in control ("dentist confirms"), which shows clinical-safety awareness.

Step 5  Sketch the key screens

Draw two boxes and narrate. Keep them rough:

"The dentist opens the patient and lands on one overview — every image together, no hunting. The system pre-flags 3 areas of interest. They tap in, see the flag pinned right on the tooth, and either confirm or dismiss it, adding a note. They move through flags one by one. In under a minute they've got their first read. I'm keeping this rough on purpose — what's your reaction to the flow?"

Step 6  Validate & wrap

"Edge cases I'd handle next: what if the AI flags nothing — I'd still make manual review easy. What if images are missing — surface that clearly so the dentist knows the picture's incomplete. I'd validate by testing with ~5 dentists and measuring time-to-first-assessment and whether they trust the flags.

To recap: I focused on the time-pressured general dentist, solved the 'scattered images, slow to spot problems' pain with a unified overview plus confirmable flags, and kept the clinician in control. With more time I'd add the prior-visit compare view."
Why this scores well: clear user, mapped workflow, prioritized ruthlessly, kept the human-in-the-loop (clinical safety), named a metric, invited the room in throughout, and showed genuine curiosity about their domain.

Question bank

About the problem & scope

  • "What problem are we really trying to solve here — and for whom?"
  • "Is there a specific platform — desktop chairside, tablet, cloud?"
  • "Any constraints I should design within — existing product, tech limits, timeline?"

About the users (lean on them as domain experts)

B · Questions they may ask you

Process & craft

  • "How do you work with developers?" → Design specs, early involvement, feasibility trade-offs — the role explicitly involves "supporting dev teams through implementation."
  • "How do you know a design worked?" → Metrics + qualitative testing. Envista is a metrics/continuous-improvement culture — name both.

  • "How do you map a complex workflow?" → This is the core skill. Talk journey maps, service blueprints, and validating each step with real users.

  • "How do you bring the voice of real dentists and labs into the design process?"

  • "What's the hardest UX challenge the team is wrestling with right now?"

Dental domain primer

You said dental is new to you — so here's enough to speak the language confidently and design credible flows. You don't need to be a clinician; you need to sound curious and informed.

The people you'd design for

🦷

General dentist

Highest-frequency user. Time-pressured, between patients. Does exams, diagnoses, fillings, crowns, refers complex cases out. Wants speed + confidence.

🔬

Specialist / surgeon

Oral surgeon, periodontist, or implantologist. Does implant surgery, complex planning. Power user of 3D planning tools. Precision-obsessed.

🛠️

Dental lab technician

Makes crowns, bridges, dentures, surgical guides from the dentist's scans/plans. Bridges digital design and physical fabrication.

📋

Assistant / practice manager

Preps rooms, manages records, scheduling, licensing. Cares about smooth admin and data being in the right place.

Don't forget the patient — the ultimate beneficiary. Better UX → faster, clearer, less stressful care. Great line to land in the room.

The workflow — imaging to treatment (the thing DTX Studio powers)

  1. Capture images: intraoral scanner (3D digital impression), 2D X-rays, CBCT (3D cone-beam CT), clinical photos.
  2. Consolidate & diagnose: bring it all together (DTX Studio Clinic), review, spot findings. AI increasingly assists here.
  3. Plan treatment: implant planning (DTX Studio Implant), orthodontics, or restorative / smile design.
  4. Fabricate: lab makes the crown/guide/appliance (DTX Studio Lab), in-house or industrial.
  5. Treat & place: surgery or restoration, sometimes with a surgical guide or 3D navigation.
  6. Follow up: monitor over time — "continuity of care" across visits.
The big UX problem DTX solves: this data lives across many devices and software. The value is connecting it into one continuous, low-friction flow — clinic ↔ specialist ↔ lab. If your whiteboard answer reduces fragmentation or friction, you're on-message.

Vocabulary cheat-sheet — enough to sound fluent

CBCT — Cone Beam Computed Tomography. A 3D X-ray of the jaw/teeth. Key for implant planning.
Intraoral scanner (IOS) — a wand that captures a 3D digital impression of the mouth (replaces goopy physical molds). DEXIS makes the IS-series.
DICOM / STL — file formats. DICOM = medical imaging (CBCT); STL = 3D surface meshes (scans, for design/printing).
Implant planning — deciding exactly where/angle/depth to place a titanium implant, using the patient's 3D anatomy + the planned tooth ("prosthetic-driven").
Surgical guide — a 3D-printed template that guides the drill during implant surgery to match the plan.
Smile design — designing the look of restored/aligned teeth, often blending a face photo with the 3D model. (A Medicim heritage strength.)
Osseointegration — the implant fusing with the jawbone over months. Why follow-up matters.
Restorative — crowns, bridges, veneers, dentures — restoring form/function of teeth.
Occlusion — how upper and lower teeth meet (the bite). Matters for any restoration.
Chairside — done right at the patient's chair during the visit (vs. planned later at a desk).
You don't need to memorize all of this. Know CBCT, intraoral scanner, implant planning, surgical guide, lab, and "continuity of care." Drop two or three naturally and ask about the rest — curiosity beats false expertise.

Curveballs & recovery

They will test how you react under pressure — often on purpose. Here's how to stay composed and turn each one into a plus.

⚡ They change the constraint mid-way

"Actually, now it's on a mobile phone." → Don't panic. "Great — that changes a few things. On a smaller screen I'd prioritize [X] and move [Y] behind a tap." Adapting live is exactly what they want to see.

🤔 "Why did you do it that way?"

Not an attack — a probe. "Good question. I chose it because [user reason]. The trade-off is [X]. An alternative would be [Y] — I leaned this way because [priority]."

🧊 You go blank

Return to the framework. "Let me step back to my user and their main goal." Or think out loud: "I want to get this right, give me a few seconds." Silence-with-a-plan is fine; frozen panic isn't.

🙅 Someone disagrees with you

"That's a fair challenge — say more?" Then integrate or respectfully hold: "I hear that; I'd weigh it against [user need]. How would you prioritize?" Collaboration > being right.

🩺 A domain objection you can't answer

"Honestly, you'd know the clinical reality better than me — how does that actually work?" Turning a gap into a collaborative question is a strength, not a fail.

⏳ You're running out of time

"I'm mindful of time, so let me prioritize the core flow and just talk through what I'd do next." Showing prioritization under pressure is a senior signal.

The three things that sink candidates — avoid all three

  • Going silent. If they can't hear your thinking, they can't reward it. Narrate constantly.
  • Jumping to pixels. Designing screens before understanding the user/problem. Spend the first third understanding.
  • Getting defensive. Treating feedback as a threat. Stay curious and open — every question is a chance to show how you collaborate.
Your mantra if nerves hit: "Think out loud. Involve the room. Stay curious." Everything else follows from those three.
